Standard Deviation and Confidence Interval

Published

November 12, 2026

The one idea to take away
  • The standard deviation (SD) describes how much individuals vary around the sample mean. It is descriptive.
  • The standard error (SE = SD/√n) describes how precisely the sample mean estimates the population mean. It is inferential and shrinks as the sample grows.
  • The confidence interval turns the SE into a range of plausible values for the population mean: mean ± multiplier × SE.

1. Standard deviation: how individuals vary

The standard deviation (SD) describes the spread of individual observations around the mean:

\[SD = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}}\]

Hover over (or tap) the formula to see what it measures

Each dot is one observation x; each arrow is its deviation from the mean (x − x̄). The SD squares these deviations (so left and right do not cancel), adds them up, divides by n − 1, and takes the square root: roughly the typical distance of an observation from the mean.

The SD is a valid measure of variability whatever the shape of the distribution. When the data are roughly normal (bell-shaped and symmetric), it also gives a simple guide to where individual values fall, known as the empirical rule:

  • About 68% of values fall within 1 SD of the mean
  • About 95% fall within 2 SD (more precisely 1.96 SD)
  • About 99.7% fall within 3 SD

Worked example (NHANES heights, the course dataset): the mean height is 167.05 cm with an SD of 10.09 cm. If height is roughly normal, about 95% of individuals are between

\[167.05 \pm 1.96 \times 10.09 = 147.3 \text{ to } 186.8 \text{ cm}\]

This range describes people, not the mean. Keep this 40 cm range in mind; we will compare it with the confidence interval later.

(The empirical rule figure is adapted from the U.S. National Library of Medicine’s statistics course on Distribution.)

2. Sample vs. population: the mean itself varies

We never measure the whole population. We take one sample and compute its mean. A different sample of the same size would give a slightly different mean, and another sample a different one again.

The simulation below draws 2,000 samples from a population of heights (mean 167 cm, SD 10 cm) and plots the mean of each sample. Choose a sample size to see how much the sample means vary.

The individual heights always have an SD of about 10 cm, whatever the sample size. The sample means, however, cluster more tightly around the true mean as n grows.

3. Standard error: the SD of the sample means

The spread of the sample means in the simulation has its own name: the standard error of the mean (SE). It is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the mean, and it can be estimated from a single sample:

\[SE = \frac{SD}{\sqrt{n}}\]

SD SE (of the mean)
What it describes spread of individual observations around the mean spread of the sample mean across repeated samples
Type descriptive inferential
Formula \(\sqrt{\sum (x-\bar{x})^2 / (n-1)}\) \(SD / \sqrt{n}\)
As n grows stays about the same (it estimates a fixed population value) shrinks
Size any never larger than the SD
Used for mean ± 1.96 SD: where ~95% of individuals fall (if roughly normal) the CI: mean ± multiplier × SE

For the NHANES heights: \(SE = 10.09 / \sqrt{15\,148} = 0.08\) cm. People differ by about 10 cm, yet with more than 15,000 people the mean is known to within a fraction of a millimetre per SE.

4. The confidence interval: built from the SE

Thanks to the Central Limit Theorem, the sample mean is approximately normally distributed for reasonably large n. A confidence interval for a mean therefore places a multiple of the SE (not the SD) around the sample mean:

\[\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1} \times SE\]

Which multiplier?

For large samples, the multiplier is the z-value of the standard normal distribution. Click the buttons to see the multiplier for common confidence levels:

Confidence level Multiplier (z)
68% 1.00
80% 1.28
90% 1.64
95% 1.96
99% 2.58

Note that 99% (z = 2.58) and the 99.7% of the empirical rule (3 SD) are different levels.

Small samples: the t distribution

The z-values assume the population SD is known. In practice we estimate it from the sample, which adds uncertainty. The correct multiplier then comes from the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom, and this is what SPSS uses. The t multiplier is larger for small samples and approaches 1.96 as n grows:

Sample size n df 95% multiplier (\(t_{n-1}\))
5 4 2.78
10 9 2.26
30 29 2.05
100 99 1.98
very large ∞ 1.96 (= z)

Worked example (NHANES heights)

\[167.05 \pm 1.96 \times 0.08 = 166.89 \text{ to } 167.21 \text{ cm}\]

Compare this with section 1: about 95% of individuals are between 147.3 and 186.8 cm, but the mean is pinned down to within about 0.3 cm. Same data, very different questions.

5. How to interpret a 95% CI

Imagine repeating the study many times and computing a 95% CI each time. About 95% of those intervals would contain the true population mean. The simulation below shows 50 samples (n = 20) from a population with a true mean of 167 cm. Intervals that miss the true mean are shown in red.

Common misinterpretations
  • ✅ Correct: “If we repeated this study many times, 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true mean.”
  • ❌ “There is a 95% probability that the true mean lies in this interval.” The true mean is fixed; this interval either contains it or not. The 95% describes the method, not one interval.
  • ❌ “95% of the data lie within the CI.” That is the job of mean ± 1.96 SD (section 1). The CI is about the mean, not about individuals.

6. Reporting: SD or SE?

  • Use mean ± SD to describe your sample (how much people vary).
  • Use the 95% CI to show the precision of an estimate.
  • Avoid “mean ± SE”. The ±1 SE interval is only a 68% CI, so it makes the mean look more precise than a 95% CI would.
  • Always state whether a “±” refers to the SD or the SE.

7. Finding them in SPSS

In SPSS (Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Explore), the output for height shows all three quantities side by side:

Statistic Value What it tells you
Mean 167.05 cm best estimate of the population mean
Std. Deviation 10.09 cm how much individuals differ
Std. Error (of the mean) 0.08 cm how precisely the mean is estimated
95% CI for Mean, lower bound 166.89 cm mean − \(t_{n-1}\) × SE
95% CI for Mean, upper bound 167.21 cm mean + \(t_{n-1}\) × SE
Summary

SD: how much individuals vary. SE = SD/√n: how precisely the sample mean estimates the population mean. CI = mean ± t × SE: the SE turned into a range of plausible values for the population mean. Larger samples shrink the SE and the CI, but not the SD.

References: Altman DG, Bland JM. Standard deviations and standard errors. BMJ 2005;331:903. Standard deviation and standard error: interpretation, usage and reporting. Med J Aust 2018;208(2). U.S. National Library of Medicine, Introduction to Statistics: Distribution.

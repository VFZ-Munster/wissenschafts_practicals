1 True or false? “An association between two variables does not mean that one causes the other.”

  1. True
  2. False
  3. I don't know
💡 True. Two variables can move together because of chance or because a third factor drives both (a confounder). In a cross-sectional study like NHANES we can describe associations, but we cannot show causes.

2 A study finds that people who drink a lot of coffee develop lung cancer more often. Is this…

  1. Causation: drinking coffee causes lung cancer
  2. Association: a third factor explains both
  3. I don't know
💡 Association. Smoking is the confounder. Heavy coffee drinkers are more often smokers, and smoking causes lung cancer. When smokers are compared with smokers and non-smokers with non-smokers, the link between coffee and lung cancer largely disappears.

Risk measures

Difference, ratio and odds answer three different questions

Risk difference RD = a/(a+b) − c/(c+d)
Relative risk RR = [a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)]
Odds ratio OR = (a/b) / (c/d) = ad / bc

Hand-check SPSS: subtract the row percentages to get RD, divide them to get RR, and compute OR from the four cells. Bars use a log scale, and the centre line marks 1 (no association).

Pearson vs Spearman

Pearson measures linear association. Spearman uses ranks, so it measures monotonic association.

Linear data
Pearson r
Spearman ρ

Use Pearson when

  • both variables are continuous,
  • the relationship is roughly linear, and
  • the distribution is not strongly skewed and has no extreme outliers.
Non-linear (monotonic) data
Pearson r
Spearman ρ

Use Spearman when you have

  • ordinal or rank-based data,
  • a monotonic relationship that may not be linear, or
  • skewed distributions or outliers.

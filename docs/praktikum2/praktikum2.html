Part 1: First Steps

When working with a new dataset, you should first familiarize yourself with its variables and codings. Examples of this are given in Exercises 1 and 2. A graphical representation, as in the following exercises, can provide a substantive overview of the data.

Exercise 1

What is the level of measurement (scale type) for Gender/sex, Body Mass Index (BMI), and BMI categories?

First, answer this question based on your knowledge from the lecture, and then check whether the variables are correctly coded in SPSS.

To do this, switch from the Data View to the Variable View.⚙️

What is the level of measurement of Gender/sex?

  • Nominal
  • Ordinal
  • Scale (metric)

Nominal – the categories (male, female) have no natural order; they are only labels.

What is the level of measurement of BMI?

  • Nominal
  • Ordinal
  • Scale (metric)

Scale – BMI is a continuous measurement in kg/m²; values can be meaningfully added, subtracted and averaged.

What is the level of measurement of BMI categories?

  • Nominal
  • Ordinal
  • Scale (metric)

Ordinal – the categories (< 18.5, 18.5–25, 25–30, ≥ 30) have a meaningful order.

How does the level of measurement (scale level) of the variable number of teeth differ from that of body height or BMI index?

Compared with BMI, number of teeth is…

  • Also continuous
  • Discrete numeric
  • Ordinal
  • Nominal

Discrete numeric – teeth can only be counted in whole numbers, while BMI is continuous and can take decimal values. Both are still quantitative, so both are Scale in SPSS.

Exercise 2

How can the following variables be appropriately represented in a graph?: Gender, mean attachment loss, number of teeth.

Decide, before entering the data, whether to use a bar chart, histogram, or boxplot.

SPSS Workflow Guide
Graphs → Chart Builder

Best graph for Gender?

  • Bar chart
  • Histogram
  • Boxplot

Bar chart only – gender is categorical, so we show the frequency of each category. Histograms and boxplots need numeric values.

Best graph for mean attachment loss?

  • Bar chart
  • Histogram
  • Boxplot

Histogram and boxplot – both work, because mean attachment loss is continuous: a histogram shows the shape of the distribution, a boxplot summarises median, spread and outliers. A bar chart is for categories.

Best graph for number of teeth?

  • Bar chart
  • Histogram
  • Boxplot

Histogram and boxplot – number of teeth is quantitative (discrete), so both work. A bar chart with one bar per count (0–32) is possible, but with so many values a histogram is clearer. Here the histogram is split by sex.

Exercise 4

Calculate the risk difference, relative risk, and odds ratio for the association between diabetes and periodontitis.

SPSS Workflow Guide
  1. Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs
  2. In the Crosstabs dialog, click Cells..., under Percentages select Row, then click Continue.
  3. Click Statistics..., check Risk, and click Continue.

Calculate manually:

Risk difference (RD)

Subtract the percentage values: \[ RD = p_{\text{diabetic w/ perio}} - p_{\text{non-diabetic w/ perio}} \]

Relative risk (RR)

Divide the percentage values: \[ RR = \frac{p_{\text{diabetic w/ perio}}}{p_{\text{non-diabetic w/ perio}}} \]

Odds ratio (OR)

The odds ratio compares the odds of periodontitis between people with diabetes and people without diabetes.

Diabetic group

\[ \text{Odds}_{\text{diabetic}} = \frac{a}{b} \]

Non-diabetic group

\[ \text{Odds}_{\text{non-diabetic}} = \frac{c}{d} \]

Final odds ratio

\[ OR = \frac{a/b}{c/d} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c} \]

Notation

Symbol Meaning
\(a\) Diabetics with periodontitis
\(b\) Diabetics without periodontitis
\(c\) Non-diabetics with periodontitis
\(d\) Non-diabetics without periodontitis

Let the 2×2 table be:

Periodontitis (+) Periodontitis (−)
Diabetic ( a ) ( b )
Non-diabetic ( c ) ( d )

Enter your results (two decimals).

  • Risk difference (RD)
  • Relative risk (RR)
  • Odds ratio (OR)

Which statement correctly describes the odds ratio you calculated?

  • It is the difference in the risk of periodontitis between the two groups, in percentage points.
  • It shows that diabetes causes periodontitis.
  • It compares the odds of periodontitis in people with diabetes with the odds in people without diabetes.
  • It tells you how many times higher the risk of periodontitis is in people with diabetes.

The OR compares odds, not risks. The risk ratio here is the RR = 1.88. The two differ because periodontitis is not rare (about 15 %). The data are cross-sectional, so the OR describes an association, not a cause.

Manual calculations

Risk difference

\[ RD = 25.6\% - 13.6\% = 12.0\% \]

Interpretation: People with diabetes have a 12 percentage-point higher observed risk of periodontitis.

Relative risk

\[ RR = \frac{25.6\%}{13.6\%} = 1.88 \]

Interpretation: Individuals with diabetes have 1.88 times the observed risk of periodontitis compared with those without diabetes.

Odds ratio

\[ OR = \frac{199 \times 6290}{578 \times 988} = 2.19 \]

Interpretation: People with diabetes have 2.19 times higher observed odds of having periodontitis.

Warning

Because these are cross-sectional data, interpret the association descriptively. It may be an apparent association and does not prove causality.

Exercise 6

Calculate the covariance and the Pearson correlation coefficient for the relationship between height and weight.

SPSS Workflow Guide
Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate → Select Pearson

Enter the Pearson correlation coefficient from your SPSS output (three decimals).

  • Pearson correlation (r)

Which statement about the Pearson correlation you found between height and weight is correct?

  • It shows a strong positive association.
  • It shows a moderate positive linear association.
  • It is the percentage of the variation in weight that is explained by height.
  • It is the increase in weight (kg) per cm of height.

r = 0.466 is a moderate, positive, linear association: taller people tend to be heavier. The percentage of variation explained is r² = 0.217, i.e. about 22 %, not 46.6 %. And r has no units, so it is not the change in kg per cm – that would be the slope of a regression line.

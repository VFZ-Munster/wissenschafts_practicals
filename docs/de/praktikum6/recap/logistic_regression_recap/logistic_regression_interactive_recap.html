Odds & Ends of Logistic Regression

The bridge from linear to logistic regression, in eight short stops. Drag the sliders — the curves talk back.

Learning goal: understand why logistic regression is used for binary outcomes, and how to interpret its coefficients.
1

Why not just use linear regression?

Start with what you already know: fit a straight line, read off predictions. For a binary outcome — Y ∈ {0, 1}The outcome only ever takes two values: 0 (event did not happen) or 1 (event happened). — that straight line runs into trouble. Push the predictor far enough in either direction and it happily predicts probabilities below 0 or above 1. Real probabilities don't do that.

  • fitted values may be below 0 or above 1;
  • residuals are not normally distributed;
  • variance is not constant;
  • classification may be unstable.

The problem isn't that a straight line can't be fitted — it's that it doesn't naturally respect the probability scale.

Probability, odds, and log-odds

Three ways to say the same thing about chance. ProbabilityHow likely the event is, from 0 (never) to 1 (always). lives on [0, 1]. OddsHow many times more likely the event is to happen than not to happen: p / (1 − p). live on [0, ∞). Log-oddsThe natural logarithm of the odds — also called the logit. stretch that out to (−∞, +∞), which is conveniently exactly the range a straight line can cover.

Reading the coefficients

A coefficient βj is the change in log-odds for a one-unit increase in xj, holding everything else constant. Exponentiate it and you get something far more speakable: an odds ratioexp(β_j) — how many times higher (or lower) the odds are, per one-unit increase in the predictor..

ORj = exp(βj)

Estimation and uncertainty

Unlike ordinary linear regression, logistic regression isn't fit by least squares — parameters come from maximum likelihoodThe parameter values that make the observed data most probable under the model. estimation. A 95% confidence interval for a coefficient is approximately βj ± 1.96·SE(βj). Exponentiate both limits to get the CI for the odds ratio — and watch it come out lopsided.

✓

Take-home messages

01

Logistic regression is designed for binary outcomes.

Two outcomes in, one probability out.

02

It models a linear relationship on the log-odds scale.

Straight line — just not on the scale you're looking at.

03

Exponentiated coefficients are odds ratios.

exp(β) turns "change in log-odds" into something you can say out loud.

04

Odds ratios should not automatically be interpreted as risk ratios.

Same OR, different real-world impact, depending on the baseline risk.

05

Predicted probabilities are often easier to communicate.

When in doubt, report the probability, not just the ratio.

06

Model evaluation depends on the goal: explanation, causal inference or prediction.

Ask what the model is for before asking if it's "good."
